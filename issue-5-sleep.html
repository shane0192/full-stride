Issue #005 Monday: Deep Dive ~6 min read

Everyone says "sleep 8 hours" like it's the whole answer. It's not. It's barely even the right question. The real story is about what happens inside those hours — specifically, how much of your sleep is deep sleep. And after 50, that number drops off a cliff in ways most people never realize until the downstream effects start showing up.

This week we dug into the research on slow-wave sleep — the phase where your brain flushes waste proteins, consolidates memories, and releases growth hormone. Turns out, the decline is steeper and starts earlier than most of us assume. And the standard advice ("just sleep more") can actually make things worse if the architecture of your sleep is broken.

The good news: there are a handful of interventions — most of them free, all of them evidence-backed — that can meaningfully move the needle. Morning light, bedroom temperature, exercise timing, and one supplement that actually has RCT data behind it. We break all of it down below.

— The Full Stride Team

Deep Dive
Sleep
Brain Health

Your Brain Is Cleaning House at Night. After 50, the Cleaning Crew Keeps Getting Smaller.

You've heard the "sleep 8 hours" advice so many times it's basically ambient noise. Here's the problem: it's not just incomplete — in some cases, it's actively misleading.

The question isn't how long you sleep. It's how much of that sleep is the right kind.

In 2004, a team led by Maurice Ohayon analyzed 65 polysomnography studies — actual electrodes on actual heads — involving 3,577 subjects. What they found about deep sleep (slow-wave sleep, or SWS) changed the conversation about aging and sleep quality entirely.

The Research

Age 30: ~20% of sleep is deep sleep. That's roughly 90–100 minutes per night.

Age 60: that figure drops to 5–8%. That's 25–40 minutes per night.

A 60–75% reduction. Not a rounding error. Nearly three-quarters of your nightly deep sleep, gone.

This would be merely annoying if deep sleep were just about feeling rested. It isn't. Deep sleep is when your glymphatic system — the brain's waste-clearance mechanism — flushes amyloid beta, the protein associated with Alzheimer's. It's when growth hormone is secreted. It's when memories consolidate. One study found that a single night of sleep deprivation eliminated the normal morning drop in CSF amyloid beta in middle-aged men. One night.

And here's what makes the Oxford research from 2022 unsettling: reduced slow-wave brain activity in adults 50–79 was linked to widespread white matter deterioration — even in people with no sleep complaints. The damage was accumulating quietly, before anyone noticed anything was wrong.

The Counterintuitive Finding

8 hours of fragmented, shallow sleep may be worse than 6.5 hours of intact deep sleep. When you increase time-in-bed without increasing deep sleep, your sleep efficiency drops. Your brain associates the bed with wakefulness. The shallow sleep stretches longer; the deep sleep doesn't follow.

Naps: under 30 minutes are associated with reduced cognitive decline risk in multiple studies. Naps of 2+ hours? A 66% higher odds ratio for cognitive impairment. The "catch up on sleep" instinct may be working against you.

What's Actually Fixable

1. Morning bright light (30 min within an hour of waking)
After 50, your circadian clock phase-advances — your body clock shifts earlier, which is why 4am wake-ups become common. Morning light delays melatonin onset by 1–2 hours, reversing this. Indoor lighting runs ~200 lux. Overcast outdoor light: 1,000–10,000 lux. Ten minutes outside doesn't cut it. Thirty minutes does.

2. Bedroom temperature: 65°F (18.3°C)
Your core body temp needs to drop 1–2°F to trigger deep sleep. Aging impairs the thermoregulation that makes this happen. A warm bath 1–2 hours before bed paradoxically helps — vasodilation moves heat to your extremities and accelerates the core temp drop. Sounds backwards. Data supports it.

3. Exercise in the afternoon (2–6pm window)
Afternoon exercise phase-delays your circadian clock, counteracting the natural phase advance. Exercise within 2 hours of bedtime raises core temperature and delays sleep onset. Timing matters more than most people realize.

4. CBT-I over sleep medication
In head-to-head RCTs, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia outperforms sleep medications on every long-term outcome. The critical detail: benzodiazepines and Z-drugs (Ambien, Lunesta) actively suppress slow-wave sleep. They sedate you. They do not improve your sleep architecture. They may be reducing the one thing you most need to protect. The American College of Physicians recommends CBT-I as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia. Sleepio and Somryst have clinical trial support.

Shelf Check

Is It Worth It? Magnesium Glycinate

One of the few sleep supplements with actual RCT evidence. But the form and dose matter.

A 2012 randomized double-blind trial (Journal of Research in Medical Sciences, n=46 older adults) found 500mg of elemental magnesium daily for 8 weeks significantly improved sleep onset time, early morning awakening, and insomnia severity vs. placebo. Serum melatonin also increased. The effect sizes are modest but real.

Use the glycinate form specifically — it's not interchangeable with magnesium oxide (mostly useless) or citrate (laxative at sleep doses). 200–400mg elemental magnesium, 30–60 minutes before bed. Roughly 50% of Americans don't meet the RDA through diet alone, which may explain why some people respond more than others.

It's not rebuilding your slow-wave sleep architecture. It's not a substitute for any of the interventions above. But if you're going to try one sleep supplement, this is the one with the most defensible evidence base and the lowest downside risk.

A note on sleep trackers

Oura Ring, WHOOP, Apple Watch — all estimate sleep stages from accelerometry and HRV, not brainwaves. A 2019 Sleep Medicine study found they mis-classify sleep stages up to 40% of the time vs. polysomnography. Useful for trends. Don't optimize obsessively around numbers with a 40% error rate.

The Number

60–75%

How much deep sleep you lose between age 30 and 60 — from ~100 minutes per night to ~30 minutes. That nightly window is when your glymphatic system flushes amyloid beta from brain tissue, when growth hormone is released, and when the day's experiences transfer into long-term memory. The question isn't whether this is happening. The question is whether you're doing the things that meaningfully slow it.

The Move

The 10-Minute Evening Wind-Down

Not a workout. The goal is the opposite of elevated heart rate. Regular evening mobility work activates the parasympathetic nervous system, lowers cortisol, and signals to your body that performance mode is over. Do this 45–60 minutes before bed.

  1. 90/90 Hip Stretch — 90 seconds per side. Sit on the floor, both knees bent at 90°. Lean into the front hip. Hold, breathe.
  2. Supine Spinal Twist — 60 seconds per side. Lie on your back, bring one knee across the body. Let gravity do the work.
  3. Child's Pose — 2 minutes. Arms extended or by your sides. Focus on extending the exhale.
  4. Legs Up the Wall — 3 minutes. Parasympathetic activation, venous return, zero effort required.

If you fall asleep on the floor during step 4, that's not a failure.

The One Thing

If you do nothing else this week...

Go outside within an hour of waking tomorrow morning. No sunglasses. Thirty minutes. That's the whole intervention.
