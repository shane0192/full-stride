Issue #001 Monday: Deep Dive ~5 min read

There's a question most people over 50 don't think to ask until something breaks: how much muscle have I actually lost? The answer is usually more than they'd guess. The science says 1-2% per year after 50 if you're not doing anything about it. A decade of that adds up fast.

This week's Deep Dive is about resistance training and aging muscle. Not the gym-bro version. The version backed by a 12-week study where people aged 55-70 reversed measurable decline in lean mass, strength, and aerobic capacity. The protocol is simple enough to fit on an index card.

We also cover the cheapest supplement with the most evidence behind it, a single exercise worth starting with this week, and why your protein intake is probably half of what it should be.

— The Full Stride Team

Deep Dive
Resistance Training
Aging

Twelve weeks to rebuild what a decade of sitting took away

The Study: Researchers at Geroscience followed 41 men and women aged 55–70 through a 12-week resistance training program — three sessions per week, plus one weekly HIIT session. They measured everything: whole-body lean mass, thigh muscle fiber composition, strength, VO2max, cholesterol, immune markers.

The Finding: Across the board, the results weren't subtle. Lean mass went up (p < 0.001). Type II muscle fibers — the fast-twitch "power fibers" your body stops maintaining if you don't use them — grew significantly. Strength improved on every measure. VO2max improved. Total cholesterol dropped.

The Part Worth Stopping For: VO2max — aerobic capacity, the single metric most consistently linked to long-term survival — improved from resistance training alone. Not from cardio. From lifting. That quietly dismantles the assumption that you need two separate routines to cover your bases.

The Context: After age 50, adults lose 1–2% of muscle mass per year without intervention. A sedentary 65-year-old has likely lost 15–30% of what they had at 50. A supporting 2026 RCT in Maturitas (n=65 postmenopausal women, 15 weeks) found ~4% muscle volume increase across all major muscle groups versus zero change in the control group. Different population. Same story.

The Takeaway: 2–3 resistance training sessions per week. Compound movements (squats, hinges, rows, presses). Start lighter than you think you need to — the goal is 12 weeks of consistency, not heroics on week one. One higher-intensity session per week. That's the whole protocol.

Keep in Mind: This was a supervised study with controlled intensity. The 12-minute vs. 45-minute finding only holds when effort is matched. Casual movement is not the same as progressive overload — but it's a better starting point than nothing.

The Shelf Check — Is It Worth It?

Creatine Monohydrate

What It Claims: Increase muscle strength, support lean mass gains, improve exercise performance.

What the Evidence Says: A 2024 meta-analysis — 20 RCTs, 1,093 participants aged 55+ — found creatine combined with resistance exercise produced +2.1 kg on the 1-rep max vs. exercise plus placebo (p=0.001). The key phrase is combined with resistance exercise. Creatine alone, without training, produced minimal benefit. It's an amplifier, not a substitute.

Who It Helps: Anyone doing consistent resistance training who wants to get more out of it. Cost: ~$5–15/month. FDA GRAS status. Decades of safety data.

Who Should Skip It: Anyone not currently training. Creatine sitting in your cabinet while you sit on your couch is expensive nothing.

Bottom Line: Three dollars a month of measurably extra strength gains is one of the better deals in this space.

Worth It (with resistance training)

The Number

27%

One hour of resistance training per week — split across two or three sessions — is associated with a 27% reduction in all-cause mortality risk. Simply starting, even inconsistently, reduces the risk by 15%. That's from a 2022 meta-analysis of 10 prospective studies covering nearly half a million adults. One hour. Per week. That's the whole ask.

The Move

This Week: The Goblet Squat

We're starting here because leg strength is where the research points first — and because most people have no idea their legs have gotten as weak as they have. Do it with a kettlebell, a dumbbell, or a full water jug.

1. Hold the weight at your chest, elbows pointing down.
2. Feet shoulder-width, toes turned out slightly.
3. Sit back and down — chest up, knees tracking over toes.
4. Drive through your heels to stand.

3 sets × 8–12 reps.

If it hurts: reduce depth — quarter-squats still build strength.

The Obvious Thing — You Know This. Are You Actually Doing It?

Protein.

This isn't new information. It's just still being ignored. Your body becomes less efficient at using dietary protein to build and maintain muscle after 50 — a process called anabolic resistance. The fix isn't complicated.

The target: 0.7–1g per pound of body weight per day. For a 170-pound person, that's 120–170g. Most adults eating a typical Western diet are hitting 60–80. That's roughly half.

One practical move: Add a protein source — eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, meat, fish — to every meal, not just dinner. Distribution across meals matters almost as much as the total. That's the whole thing. Boring, effective, underused.
